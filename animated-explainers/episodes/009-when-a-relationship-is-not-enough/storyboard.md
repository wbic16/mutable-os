# When a relationship is not enough

*Volume III — The Architecture of Meaning · Concept 009*

The three Michelangelo sentences are fictional teaching examples. The letters A and B distinguish two assertions of the same type.

## 00–07 seconds · A familiar relationship

The Volume III title gives way to an illustrative sentence. A single directed link labelled “Lived in” joins Michelangelo and Florence.

**Caption**  Two entities. One relationship.

**0.75 s**  A relationship between two entities is a familiar way of representing knowledge.

## 07–17 seconds · A more complex sentence

Read the first sending. Four teal marks grow beneath the original words as the participants appear. The sentence remains intact.

**Caption**  Multiple participants. One assertion.

**7.25 s**  But even a simple sentence can describe something more complex. Michelangelo sent money to his brothers from Rome. Several participants belong to a single assertion.

## 17–26 seconds · Three independent links

The links from Michelangelo to Money, His brothers and Rome appear in sequence. Their verbs still read plausibly.

**Caption**  What holds the relationships together?

**17.40 s**  We could represent this as several separate relationships. But how do we preserve the fact that they belong together?

## 26–39 seconds · A possible pairing

The second sending introduces A letter, A friend and Florence. Six links now meet at the same sender. A coral question asks whether the money went to the friend. The dashed arc marks a possible reading.

**Caption**  The relationships survive. Their grouping may not.

**26.40 s**  Add another sending, involving a different object, recipient and place.

**31.60 s**  Without preserving the original grouping, we may no longer know which participants belong to which assertion.

## 39–53 seconds · The statement becomes an object

Return to the first passage. Three connectors change their point of attachment as Claim A emerges. Sender, Theme, Recipient and Origin give each participant a role in this sending. Theme is glossed as “the thing sent”.

**Caption**  The statement becomes a first-class object.

**39.45 s**  Instead, we can represent the assertion itself as an object: a Claim Statement.

**46.00 s**  Each participant connects to it through a particular semantic role.

## 53–65 seconds · Two statements of one type

Claim B takes its place below Claim A. Each has its own object, recipient and origin. Both Sender connections lead to the same Michelangelo. Once both groups are visible, introduce “n-ary statement”.

**Caption**  Same statement type. Different statements.

**53.20 s**  Both statements describe sending, and both share a sender. Each retains its own identity.

**59.35 s**  An n-ary statement brings several participants together in one assertion.

## 65–75 seconds · Back to the passages

Follow the teal evidence connections from Claim A to Document 02 and Claim B to Document 03. Two measured strokes per passage trace the complete sentence across its display line break.

**Caption**  Statements retain textual provenance.

**65.45 s**  And because the statement has its own identity, it can retain a connection to the passage from which it was derived.

## 75–83 seconds · Questions about Claim A

Four questions address the statement itself. They leave its date, attribution, evidence and circumstances open.

**Caption**  The assertion becomes an object of inquiry.

**75.40 s**  The statement itself can now become something we identify, qualify, question and investigate.

## 83–90 seconds · Closing card

A miniature of Claim A and its four participants dissolves into the engraved leaves as the first closing line fills the bookend. “Meaning is not always binary.” appears beneath it with the second spoken cue.

**83.20 s**  A statement is more than the sum of its relationships.

**87.40 s**  Meaning is not always binary.
